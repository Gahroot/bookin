## What was the manager being asked to stand behind?

At Black Mountain Fund, a manager questioned the acquisition calculations and asked whether the reviews should go to partners for testing.[^E1][^E3]

That is a consequential moment in a review: someone has to decide whether the comparison is ready for the people responsible for the acquisition decision. The acceptance standard should be specific. A manager should be able to explain what each scenario represents and which assumptions deserve scrutiny before the discussion moves to a recommendation.

The business problem was confidence in the basis for the comparison. I want the question “Are these calculations correct?” addressed directly. An executive deserves an answer about the substance of the review, with enough detail to judge the response.

For a fund executive, this is a useful place to set a firm standard. Presentation quality should never be the acceptance criterion for decision support. Ask the person responsible for the review why the comparison deserves to enter the partner discussion. Readiness to circulate should mean something concrete about the calculations and the assumptions behind them.

## What did I change in the comparison?

I corrected the assumed-loan financing treatment where the loan balance and interest rate came from separate deal documents.[^E2][^E5]

For a partner, the relevant question is which borrowing arrangement the acquisition assumes. The standard should be a coherent financing assumption, supported by the material describing the arrangement being evaluated. The organisation of the documents should have no authority over the business definition of the loan.

I also corrected investor fee treatment in the acquisition scenarios.[^E4][^E5]

The acceptance question here belongs at the investor level: does the scenario apply the intended charges when presenting the acquisition? Before accepting a comparison, require an explanation of whose perspective it represents. Give fee treatment the same attention as the headline assumptions in the investment case.

I changed the sale timing to coincide with the assumed loan’s maturity.[^E4][^E5]

An executive reviewing an exit assumption should ask what event determines the end of the scenario and whether that event matches the intended case. Treat the sale date as an explicit decision assumption. Require a business explanation for the date selected.

I added a purchase option using the planned financing.[^E4][^E5]

A comparison should make the intended alternatives explicit. The acceptance question is simple: does the review represent the option management actually wants to evaluate? Require the person presenting it to describe that option in ordinary business language. A scenario label alone should never be the explanation.

These are the kinds of details I want discussed before anyone asks partners to choose. The executive standard should be consistent across the review: explain the assumption, explain its treatment and be precise about the question the scenario is meant to answer.

## What was different when the work was complete?

The completion record confirms that the corrections and the added purchase option were live.[^E5]

A release confirmation belongs in an executive account of the work. Ask for a clear distinction between a requested change, work under review and a completed release. Keep responsibility for that distinction explicit through the final handoff.

The closing question should be narrow: what evidence supports calling the work complete? Keep that separate from whether the acquisition itself is attractive. Approval of a system change and approval of an investment deserve separate decisions, each with its own basis.

For this case, the proper boundary is the decision-support system. Investment performance and partner acceptance require their own evidence. Neither should be implied by a delivery record. I want the language used to describe an accomplishment to carry exactly the weight the record supports.

## What should you ask of your own organisation or vendors?

Start with the moment a review leaves the person who prepared it. Who is responsible for saying it is ready? What does that person need to see before making that call? Put those questions into the conversation before asking for another feature.

Ask for explanations at the level of the business decision. Your team should be able to describe what an acquisition scenario assumes without making the partner discussion depend on specialist terminology. Require the same clarity from a vendor. A statement that a calculation has been updated is an incomplete account until the business owner understands which treatment was intended.

Then ask how completion will be demonstrated. Agree on the evidence that belongs in the handoff and who will assess it. Keep the scope of acceptance precise: which concern was raised, which change addresses it and what remains for the people making the investment decision to judge?

Give the person questioning the review a clear place in that process. Treat a challenge to the calculations as a request for a substantive answer. Ask the team to respond in terms the reviewer can assess, and keep unresolved questions visible to whoever owns the decision.

That is the standard I bring to this work: a defined business question, an explicit account of the change and evidence for the outcome being claimed. If your acquisition reviews are approaching a partner discussion, book a call to talk through what your team needs to stand behind.
